The ongoing crisis in Cameroon's Anglophone regions has disrupted formal education systems, leaving thousands of children out of school. Yet in communities adapting to conflict and uncertainty, an innovative approach is gaining momentum: **playful peer tutoring**—where children teach each other through games, collaborative learning, and structured play. This approach simultaneously addresses educational needs, psychosocial wellbeing, and social cohesion.

## The Crisis Context

The conflict in the Northwest and Southwest regions has:

- **Closed schools** – Insecurity prevents many institutions from operating regularly
- **Displaced students** – Families flee violence, leaving children with no formal educational access
- **Traumatized learners** – Exposure to conflict creates psychological burden that traditional classrooms struggle to address
- **Community fragmentation** – Displacement and fear disrupt social bonds and community structures

When traditional education collapses, children face not just educational loss—they face psychological vulnerability, increased risk of exploitation, and erosion of hope for the future.

## Playful Peer Tutoring: What It Is

Playful peer tutoring is an educational approach where:

- **Older or more capable students** teach younger or struggling learners
- **Learning happens through games, songs, stories, and activities** rather than formal lectures
- **Play becomes the medium** for learning literacy, numeracy, and life skills
- **Peer relationships form the foundation** of teaching and support

Rather than waiting for formal schools to reopen, playful peer tutoring enables:

- **Immediate educational continuity** in informal settings
- **Flexible scheduling** adapted to security and family circumstances
- **Psychological safety** through trusted peer relationships
- **Culturally relevant** learning that connects to community life

## Why This Works in Crisis Contexts

### 1. **Psychological Resilience Through Play**

Play is therapeutic. In crisis contexts, children experience trauma, anxiety, and fear. Playful learning:

- Provides emotional relief and psychological regulation
- Creates moments of normalcy and joy
- Builds agency through participation and choice
- Fosters peer support and reduced isolation

Research consistently shows that play-based learning during emergencies supports trauma recovery and psychological wellbeing.

### 2. **Educational Continuity Without Formal Structures**

When schools cannot operate:

- Peer tutoring requires no specialized facilities
- Sessions can happen anywhere—under trees, in community centers, in homes
- Learning adapts to available resources and time
- Multiple small learning groups can operate simultaneously

This flexibility means that even amid insecurity, learning continues.

### 3. **Peer Relationships as Protective Factors**

Peer tutoring strengthens protective relationships:

- **Tutors gain agency and responsibility** – Teaching gives purpose and confidence
- **Learners build trust and connection** – Peer support is often less intimidating than adult instruction
- **Reciprocal learning** – Tutees sometimes teach tutors, creating mutual respect
- **Social cohesion** – Shared learning builds community bonds across dividing lines

These relationships create resilience—research shows that supportive peer and adult relationships are the strongest predictors of recovery from trauma.

### 4. **Culturally Responsive Learning**

Playful peer tutoring can incorporate:

- **Local languages and dialects** rather than imposing formal linguistic standards
- **Community stories and wisdom** that connect learning to identity
- **Cultural values** around cooperation, respect, and collective wellbeing
- **Locally relevant skills** including agricultural knowledge, conflict resolution, and traditional crafts

This cultural grounding makes learning meaningful and affirms identity during periods of cultural threat.

### 5. **Inclusion and Accessibility**

Unlike formal schools that may exclude children based on age, language, or background, playful peer tutoring can:

- **Adapt pacing and style** to individual learner needs
- **Welcome all children** regardless of prior schooling
- **Create multiple entry points** through different activities and peer groups
- **Reduce cost barriers** that prevent school attendance

## Supporting Evidence

Educational research and crisis response experience demonstrate that:

- **Play-based learning enhances retention** of academic content compared to traditional instruction
- **Peer teaching improves both tutors' and learners' achievement**
- **Playful approaches support psychosocial wellbeing** in crisis contexts
- **Community-based education fills gaps** when formal systems collapse
- **Flexibility and informality increase access** for marginalized children

## What Communities Can Do

If you're supporting children in crisis-affected regions:

- **Encourage peer teaching** – Recognize older children's potential as educators
- **Facilitate safe play spaces** – Protect time and places where learning through play can happen
- **Provide materials** – Simple resources like chalk, paper, and rope enable diverse play-based learning
- **Listen to children** – Let children guide what they want to learn and how they want to learn it
- **Connect with organizations** like INADESU that can provide training and support

## The Path Forward

The Anglophone crisis is ongoing, and formal education may remain disrupted for years. Rather than viewing this as purely a loss, communities are discovering the power of educational approaches that put children's agency, creativity, and peer relationships at the center.

Playful peer tutoring is not a replacement for formal education—it's a bridge. It maintains learning during crisis while simultaneously supporting psychological wellbeing and community resilience. When formal schools reopen, children who have learned through play often have stronger foundational skills, better social-emotional development, and greater confidence.
